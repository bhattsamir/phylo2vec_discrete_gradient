               Exact balanced minimum evolution evaluation
                and parallel search in Phylo2Vec coordinates



                                              Abstract
         Balanced minimum evolution (BME) provides a distance-based criterion for phylogenetic
     inference, but searching large tree spaces requires repeated evaluation of many candidate
     rearrangements. We develop a shared evaluator that computes all valid single-coordinate
     BME changes for an N -leaf tree in an ordered Phylo2Vec representation in O(N 2 ) work and
     supports parallel GPU execution. For larger trees, we optimize the connections between fixed
     components on a smaller skeleton tree. Exact balanced averages of the original distances
     make the skeleton objective equal to the full-tree objective up to a constant. Every skeleton
     change therefore gives the corresponding full-tree BME change, with an O(K 2 ) coordinate
     sweep once the distances between its K components are available. Independent CPU checks
     agree with direct BME differences to maximum absolute discrepancies of 3.553 × 10−14 for
     coordinate evaluation and 1.564 × 10−13 for condensation. In 120 small-tree comparisons, the
     CPU optimizer matches or improves the reference BME score in 119 cases. A full coordinate
     sweep at N = 40,000 takes 158.36 ms on eight H100 GPUs. Exactness refers to preservation of
     the specified BME objective, with numerical rounding in its evaluation and heuristic selection
     of the search trajectory.


1    Introduction
Distance-based phylogenetic inference first estimates pairwise evolutionary distances and then
searches for a tree that explains them. Balanced minimum evolution (BME) scores a topology using
a weighted sum of those distances, with weights determined by the branching structure along each
leaf-to-leaf path [1, 2, 3]. The computational challenge is to evaluate many possible rearrangements
repeatedly as the tree changes. Classical subtree-prune-and-regraft (SPR) recurrences already
permit quadratic-time neighborhood sweeps [4, 5]. At large scales, their practical cost depends on
how calculations are shared, how distance tables are stored, and how work is distributed across
processors.
    For a tree with N leaves, we organize BME search around a table of coordinate changes in the
Phylo2Vec representation. Each entry gives the change in the objective when one integer in the
tree’s encoding is replaced. The table is evaluated through shared subtree-distance calculations,
exposing independent work for parallel execution. Relabeling the current tree changes which
rearrangements are represented by single-coordinate updates while preserving the tree and its
objective.
    The same evaluator operates on a smaller tree when some internal structure is held fixed. We
replace each fixed component by one skeleton leaf and retain its contribution through an exact
balanced average of the original distances. The full objective then equals the skeleton objective
plus a constant that depends only on the fixed components. Consequently, every change evaluated
on the skeleton has the same BME change in the full tree. We call this block of component
connections a minibatch: it restricts the structures being optimized while retaining all leaves in
the objective calculation.

    We develop three algorithmic contributions:

    1. Shared coordinate evaluation. All (N − 1)2 valid entries are computed in O(N 2 ) work,
       matching the size of the explicit table. A map from coordinate values to regraft edges avoids
       separately scoring candidate trees.

    2. Exact block evaluation. The same computation applies to a K-component skeleton
       in O(K 2 ) work after its exact distances are available. The reduction includes the entire
       complement of a subtree window and specifies the updates required between windows.

    3. Parallel execution. Independent distance columns and SPR rows support GPU distribution
       and selection of the best update in each row. Reconstructing rows when needed reduces the
       balanced-distance table from approximately 4N 2 to N 2 entries while retaining quadratic
       sweep work.

    The contribution builds on established representation and BME results. Phylo2Vec intro-
duced coordinate differences and ordered relabeling [6, Section 1.3.2], and its ordered coordinate
neighborhood is a subset of the SPR neighborhood. Our evaluator retains the quadratic sweep
complexity of classical BME SPR calculations. Balanced subtree averages [2, 3] and the fixed-clade
reduction of the BME polytope [10, Section 2.4] provide the basis for exact skeleton evaluation.
We state the objective identity and prove the conditions needed by the block algorithm.
    GradME uses a cubic-time recurrence and automatic differentiation to optimize an expected
BME objective over ordered coordinates [7]. Here evaluation is specialized to a discrete tree,
including its full coordinate range. Appendix B.4 gives the connection to GradME’s vertex
derivatives. Parallel taxon-addition BME [8] and learned SPR selection [9] address tree construction
and search policy, respectively. Our experiments assess coordinate accuracy, CPU optimization,
and GPU sweep throughput.


2     Methods
2.1    Overview
The method takes a tree and a fixed matrix of distances between its leaves. A full-tree sweep
computes the objective change for every valid coordinate replacement of the current encoding. A
minibatch sweep applies the same evaluator to a skeleton of fixed components (Figure 1). After
selecting and accepting a skeleton update, the components are expanded back into the full tree
before the next block is constructed. The full-tree case is recovered by taking every component to
be a single leaf.
    Throughout, exact means that the calculated expression preserves the BME objective for the
supplied distance matrix. The distances themselves may be estimates, numerical evaluation uses
floating-point arithmetic, and the search for an optimal topology remains heuristic.

2.3     Quadratic-time coordinate evaluation
For two distinct vertices u, w, define A(u, w) as the balanced average distance between the two
components oriented away from the path joining u and w. At an internal vertex, the two outward
branches each receive weight 1/2. At a leaf, the corresponding distribution is concentrated on
that leaf. Consequently, when the component at u has children a, b,

                                       A(u, w) = 21 {A(a, w) + A(b, w)}.                                          (4)


                                                           3
The direction is important when one vertex is an ancestor of the other in a computational rooting.
In that case, the component away from w combines the sibling branch and the parent branch:

                         A(u, w) = 21 {A(sibu (w), w) + A(parent(u), w)}.                       (5)

At the degree-three computational root, the analogous average uses the two branches not containing
w. Bottom-up and top-down passes construct the required table in O(N 2 ) arithmetic operations.
    For a fixed pruned component, the standard BME SPR recurrence propagates score differences
across regraft edges in O(N ) time [4]. Each propagation step is an adjacent interchange of
four directed components. Its score change is a linear combination of their balanced distances,
with coefficient 1/4. Successive steps accumulate the change relative to the original attachment.
Phylo2Vec supplies the mapping from each valid coordinate value to one of these regraft edges.
The explicit mapping, including the collapsed-root boundary, is in Appendix B.3.

Proposition 2.1 (Cost and scope of a coordinate sweep). Given an ordered baseline and its leaf
permutation, all entries in Equation (3) can be computed in O(N 2 ) time. In exact arithmetic,
these entries equal the corresponding full-tree BME differences.

Proof. The balanced-distance table costs O(N 2 ). For each of O(N ) coordinates, pruning its
associated component and traversing the two remaining half-trees visits O(N ) edges. The SPR
recurrence evaluates the score at each edge, and the coordinate mapping selects the appropriate
values without evaluating the candidate trees independently. The mapping costs at most O(N )
per row. The recurrence preserves the Pauplin score difference at each traversal step, so selecting
a mapped edge gives Equation (3).

    The proposition concerns the coordinate neighborhood of the current labeling. It does not
assert that this neighborhood is the entire SPR neighborhood, or that coordinate descent finds a

2.6    Search strategy
The full-tree CPU optimizer evaluates coordinate differences, ranks improving rows, and filters
candidate batches for conflicts involving pruned components and attachment endpoints. It then
evaluates the combined candidate tree and backtracks to a smaller batch if the score does not
improve. The acceptance decision uses the joint objective rather than the sum of individual
predicted improvements. When the current coordinate neighborhood is exhausted, the optimizer
changes the ordered labeling and searches again. Its full-tree configuration also scans remaining
unrooted SPR moves before declaring SPR-local convergence. An optional restart stage applies
random perturbations and resumes descent while retaining the best tree found. Only accepted
descent updates and the retained best score are monotone. The perturbed working trajectory
need not be.
   An exact minibatch search can alternate skeletons at different scales. A global skeleton
rearranges the backbone, local windows expose detailed structure, and overlapping or paired
windows propose changes between regions. Each window follows four steps:

   1. Select fixed components from the current tree, including an oriented outside component for
      a proper subtree.

   2. Form the exact condensed distance matrix in Equation (7).

   3. Evaluate skeleton coordinate changes using Corollary 2.3, and check the selected update
      using Equation (11).

   4. Commit an improving candidate, graft the components back, and update summaries before
      selecting or evaluating the next window.

Each accepted update decreases the same objective under the assumptions above. Which improving
moves can be reached depends on the window and relabeling schedule, so this procedure supplies
no global-convergence guarantee.

2.7    Parallel evaluation and computational cost
Equation (4) has an independent recurrence for each fixed column. Assigning columns to threads
lets neighboring threads access consecutive entries while following the same traversal order. The
top-down correction for column w follows its ancestor path. Depth-first entry intervals determine

the next child without a traversal stack. Its total work over columns is O( w depth(w)), bounded
by O(N 2 ) and reduced to O(N log N ) for trees of logarithmic height.

retains the internal-to-leaf block and reconstructs required internal rows on demand. This reduces
that table to approximately N 2 entries, while the input matrix and other buffers still contribute
to total memory. A row-wise reduction can return the best coordinate replacement in each row

4     Results
4.1     Agreement with independent objective calculations
Table 2 compares coordinate and condensed changes with independent objective calculations.
The largest discrepancy in the CPU coordinate tests is 3.553 × 10−14 , and the condensation tests
agree to 1.564 × 10−13 . These are observed absolute errors on the tested cases, not universal
error bounds. Rooted-vector round trips recover all 1,000 tested vectors at N = 40. Unrooted
round trips preserve the split sets in a further 1,000 cases. Exhaustive rooted-tree enumeration at
N = 4, . . . , 7 checks 11,460 encodings.
    Independent enumeration also identifies the scope of the coordinate neighborhood. On
30 random trees at each tested size from N = 8 to 20, a single ordered orientation covers
approximately 27–33% of distinct SPR neighbors. Two orientations together cover approximately
52–56%. Neither constitutes an exhaustive SPR search. The full CPU optimizer’s additional SPR
scan is therefore material: independent enumeration found no improving SPR move in 90 returned
trees at N = 10, 16, 24. This observation concerns that tested configuration, not the minibatch
variant.

4.2     CPU optimization
Both methods recover the reference topology in all 60 noiseless trials. On the 60 noisy trials,
Phylo2Vec obtains two lower BME scores, 57 ties, and one higher score than the in-memory
reference. Thus it matches or improves the reference objective in 119 of 120 cases overall. A better
BME score need not imply a smaller RF distance when the input distances are noisy. For example,
the N = 200 noisy comparison has a lower mean reference RF error despite one BME-score win
for Phylo2Vec.
    At N = 200, the noiseless Apple M5 comparison requires 63.6 coordinate sweeps on average,
versus 211.0 reference SPR iterations. The corresponding mean times are 18.14 and 75.46 ms.
Table 3 gives the largest-size timings for both processors. Supplementary Tables 5 and 6 give
results at all sizes. External FastME comparisons at the same five sizes produce identical final
unrooted splits and BME differences below 10−8 in the reported checks. These checks support the
reference implementation on those inputs without making the in-memory timings measurements
of the external executable.

4.3   Coordinate sweep throughput and memory
At N = 4,000, a full coordinate sweep takes 6.219 ms on one H100, compared with 701.375 ms for
its CPU reference. At N = 40,000, distributing the full-table computation across eight H100s
reduces time from 816.34 to 158.36 ms, a factor of 5.15. The SPR stage alone falls from 756.26 to
98.28 ms. Column tiling reduces storage at the cost of recomputation: the reported one-GPU
memory requirement falls from 31,142 to 13,614 MB, while the eight-GPU tiled evaluation takes
1,032.83 ms at this size. Thus the reduced-memory variant is useful when capacity, rather than
latency, is limiting.
    Distributed and streamed configurations extend a single full-tree sweep to N = 380,000, taking
205.450 s with 72,946 MB reported per GPU on eight H100s. All these times measure evaluation
at a fixed tree.
    For the separate embedding workload with N = 10,000,000 and K = 20,000, average conden-
sation and coordinate-sweep times are 10.20 and 48.57 ms per batch on eight H100s. The reported
epoch estimate, 39.20 s, is the sum of those stage times multiplied by 667 batches. It measures
the projected cost of those stages over the prescribed blocks, excluding additional search work
and optimization to convergence. The complete scaling tables are in Appendix H.


5     Discussion
The method separates the size of a search block from the objective it evaluates. A skeleton can
optimize connections among a few components while retaining the contribution of every original
leaf through exact condensed distances. The same coordinate evaluator therefore supports both
full-tree sweeps and restricted block searches. This links the established BME component identity
to a common computational interface for parallel tree search.
     The main computational tradeoff is between reuse and reconstruction. Dense tables permit
rapid sweeps but require quadratic memory. Column tiling trades memory for repeated arithmetic.
Skeletons reduce the dimension of the optimization problem, but exact construction of their
distances can retain a dependence on the full leaf set. This becomes particularly visible in
nonlinear sequence-distance refinement and in candidates involving large condensed components.
Bilinear embedding benchmarks therefore establish a different scaling regime from the sequence
experiments.
     The experiments support faster coordinate evaluation and competitive CPU optimization on
the tested instances. Their scope leaves three limitations. First, the tested relabeling and block
schedules do not establish exhaustive coverage or global convergence, and the total number of
sweeps remains instance-dependent. Second, the numerical checks give observed discrepancies
rather than universal error bounds, and a lower BME score under estimated distances need not
imply a more accurate topology. Third, the large sequence comparisons use approximate proposals
with full-objective acceptance and do not report replicate uncertainty at the largest sizes. Those
measurements do not establish the end-to-end cost of a sequence search using exact condensed
distances for every proposal.


6     Conclusion
Shared coordinate evaluation and exact component reduction give a common basis for full-tree
and block BME search. A quadratic sweep evaluates all valid coordinate updates, and the same
computation on an exactly condensed skeleton preserves their full-tree objective changes. The
resulting parallel algorithms let a search trade the scope of its candidate set against evaluation
cost while retaining the specified BME objective.

B     Ordered representation and coordinate mapping
B.1    Linear-time decoding
For vi ≤ i, initialize a component-root array by r[x] = x. Process i = N − 2, . . . , 0. At step i,
join the components with roots r[vi ] and r[i + 1] at a new node m = 2N − 2 − i, then replace
r[vi ] by m. Before this step, the active components have distinct minimum labels 0, . . . , i + 1.
Joining the two specified components removes minimum label i + 1 and preserves minimum label
vi . Induction therefore establishes the invariant and the correctness of the construction. Each step
uses constant work, giving an O(N ) decoder. This argument depends on the ordered condition
and does not apply unchanged to a general vector with vi > i.

B.2    Relabeling a decoded tree
Proposition B.1 (Ordered representation after relabeling). Given a decoded rooted binary
tree, a parent-before-child ordering of its internal nodes constructs an ordered vector and a leaf
permutation in O(N ) time. The represented labeled tree and its BME score are unchanged.

Proof. Give the root temporary label zero and initialize a counter t = 1. At each internal node,
choose an ordering (a, b) of its children. Pass the node’s label to a, give b label t, record vt−1 as
the inherited label, and increment t. Each newly issued label follows a unique chain of inheriting


                                                     11
children to a leaf. Every leaf receives exactly one label, so the resulting labels form a permutation
of 0, . . . , N − 1.
    The inherited label was issued at an ancestor and is smaller than t, hence vt−1 ≤ t − 1. To
verify decoding, process the recorded splits in reverse order. All descendants of a node were
assigned their new labels later than that node, so both child components have already been
assembled when its recorded merge is reached. The merge therefore recreates that node’s original
clade. Induction reconstructs the entire tree. Permuting both axes of D by the inverse leaf-label
map preserves all terms in Equation (1). Each vertex is processed a constant number of times.

    The implementation chooses the child order from the minimum original leaf label in each
child component, with a second orientation reversing that choice. These are two deterministic
coordinate systems for the same current tree. Their existence proves representational coverage
after suitable relabeling, not coverage by a particular optimizer trajectory. For general candidate
vectors, the implementation also uses a tree-to-vector encoder. Its unrooted version first inserts a
degree-two root on the edge incident to leaf zero, so round-trip correctness is assessed by unrooted
splits rather than root placement.

C     Parallel dependencies and workspace
C.1    Column ownership and ancestor traversal
For a fixed column w, the bottom-up update in Equation (4) reads only earlier entries of that
column. Thus the recurrence has no cross-column dependency once its initial entries are available.
The implementation separates the necessary transpose from the recurrence rather than writing
both triangular copies concurrently. Top-down correction visits ancestors of w in order from the
computational root. If a node’s children have contiguous depth-first intervals, comparing the entry
time of w with their interval boundary selects the child containing w. Only the current node and
the fixed target are required as traversal state. The initial degree-three root update uses the other
two root branches.

C.2    Autonomous row reconstruction
Store the balanced distances from internal vertices to leaves. For a requested row w, these entries
supply its leaf values. For leaf w, the leaf values instead come directly from D. Average child
entries bottom-up to fill internal positions, then overwrite ancestors of w top-down using the
sibling and parent directions. Initialize the degree-three root correction explicitly and set the
diagonal to zero.
    For an internal position u in a disjoint component, the bottom-up average is the required
balanced distance by linearity. For a descendant u of w, the stored leaf values already use the
component at w directed away from that descendant, so averaging within the descendant gives the
correct value. Ancestors of w require the complementary direction and are supplied by the top-
down overwrite. These cases exhaust the row. The reconstruction costs O(N + depth(w)) = O(N ).
Reconstructing a constant number of rows per pruned component therefore retains quadratic
sweep complexity. The approximately fourfold reduction concerns the balanced-distance table
only.

C.3    Traversal stack size
When a traversal reaches two outward components, push the larger component first and the
smaller second on a last-in-first-out stack. The smaller component is processed next. Each extra
pending larger component is associated with descent into a component at most half the size of
its predecessor. Hence the number of simultaneously pending components is logarithmic in the
number of tree vertices. Including the active traversal state gives a safe bound ⌊log2 (2N − 2)⌋ + 2.
This is a workspace bound, not a statement that the traversal visits fewer vertices.

E     Truncation and stale outside-distance counterexamples
Two constructions illustrate the need for exact condensed distances and current outside information.
The first concerns the approximate-proposal variant in Appendix D. On a 4,102-leaf tree containing
a balanced 4,096-leaf component, an adversarial split distance can give a capped proposal change
of −0.25 while its true full-profile change is +0.2421875 for a cap of 64, or +0.234375 for a cap of
128. The proposal sign alone is therefore insufficient. Full-profile acceptance rejects both changes.
    Separately, consider two disjoint windows whose internal rearrangements change their root-to-
leaf weight vectors from pA , pB to pA + δpA , pB + δpB . With a fixed path coefficient κ between their
attachment points, their cross contribution contains κp⊤  A DAB pB . The jointly changed contribution
includes the additional term
                                            κ δp⊤A DAB δpB ,

which is absent from the sum of two changes computed against the original surroundings. In a
1,540-leaf test with two 768-leaf windows, changes −0.0032945313 and −0.0254489835 computed
against frozen surroundings combine to an actual increase of +0.0505258348. Rebuilding the
outside profile after the first commit makes the other proposal positive: +0.0538203660 or
+0.0759748183, depending on the processing order. It is then rejected. These values come from
controlled CPU replay of the acceptance and window-controller paths. They do not constitute a
native-GPU concurrency test.
