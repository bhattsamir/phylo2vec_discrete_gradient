               Exact balanced minimum evolution evaluation
                and parallel search in Phylo2Vec coordinates



                                                 Abstract
         Balanced minimum evolution (BME) provides a distance-based criterion for phylogenetic
     inference, but searching large tree spaces requires repeated evaluation of many candidate
     rearrangements. We formulate exact BME evaluation as discrete coordinate differences in
     Phylo2Vec space and organize the underlying subtree-distance recurrences for parallel execution.
     For a tree represented in an ordered labeling, all valid single-coordinate changes are evaluated in
     O(N 2 ) time. Relabeling preserves the current tree while changing the coordinate neighborhood.
     The same evaluator applies to smaller skeleton trees whose leaves represent fixed components of
     the full tree. We prove that exact balanced distances between these components preserve every
     skeleton-induced BME change. The reduced objective differs from the full objective only by a
     constant, so each minibatch coordinate difference is the corresponding full-tree difference. This
     reduction also covers a subtree window when its entire complement is retained as an oriented
     component, with outside distances updated after each committed window. Independent
     small-tree checks agree with direct BME differences to maximum absolute discrepancies of
     3.553 × 10−14 for coordinate evaluation and 1.564 × 10−13 for condensation. In 120 small-tree
     comparisons, the CPU optimizer matches or improves the reference BME score in 119 cases.
     A full gradient sweep at N = 40,000 takes 158.36 ms on eight H100 GPUs. Exactness is an
     algebraic property relative to the specified distance matrix. Numerical evaluation remains
     subject to floating-point rounding, and the search remains heuristic.


1    Introduction
Distance-based phylogenetic inference separates the estimation of pairwise evolutionary distances
from the search for a tree. Balanced minimum evolution (BME) scores a topology using a weighted
sum of those distances, with weights determined by the branching structure along each leaf-to-leaf
path [1, 2, 3]. Efficient recurrences permit an entire subtree-prune-and-regraft (SPR) neighborhood
to be evaluated in quadratic time [4, 5]. For large trees, the practical costs include storing the
subtree-distance table, repeatedly traversing the tree, and coordinating many small candidate
evaluations. The number of optimization sweeps depends on the instance and the search strategy.
    Phylo2Vec represents rooted binary trees by bounded integer vectors [6]. This representation
provides a coordinate system in which a discrete difference records the change in BME after
replacing one vector entry. An ordered labeling permits efficient decoding and maps coordinate
changes to a structured collection of SPR moves. The representation also separates the tree
topology from its leaf-label permutation. Changing the permutation can expose different coordinate
moves without changing the current labeled tree or its score.
    Our method combines this coordinate formulation with parallel evaluation and component-
based reduction. The reduction relies on established BME structure. Balanced subtree averages
underlie earlier distance-based algorithms [2, 3], and fixed-clade families have been described as
affine images of smaller BME polytopes [7, Section 2.4]. We state the corresponding objective
identity explicitly because its assumptions determine when minibatch optimization evaluates the
full-tree objective. In particular, condensed distances must include the balanced contributions of
every leaf, and a subtree window must retain the rest of the tree through the correct attachment
orientation.

2.2   Quadratic-time coordinate evaluation
For two distinct vertices u, w, define A(u, w) as the balanced average distance between the two
components oriented away from the path joining u and w. At an internal vertex, the two outward
branches each receive weight 1/2. At a leaf, the corresponding distribution is concentrated on
that leaf. Consequently, when the component at u has children a, b,

                                   A(u, w) = 21 {A(a, w) + A(b, w)}.                                (4)

The direction is important when one vertex is an ancestor of the other in a computational rooting.
In that case, the component away from w combines the sibling branch and the parent branch:

                          A(u, w) = 21 {A(sibu (w), w) + A(parent(u), w)}.                          (5)

At the degree-three computational root, the analogous average uses the two branches not containing
w. Bottom-up and top-down passes construct the required table in O(N 2 ) arithmetic operations.
    For a fixed pruned component, the standard BME SPR recurrence propagates score differences
across regraft edges in O(N ) time [4]. Each propagation step is an adjacent interchange of
four directed components. Its score change is a linear combination of their balanced distances,
with coefficient 1/4. Successive steps accumulate the change relative to the original attachment.
Phylo2Vec supplies the mapping from each valid coordinate value to one of these regraft edges.
The explicit mapping, including the collapsed-root boundary, is in Appendix A.3.
Proposition 2.1 (Cost and scope of a coordinate sweep). Given an ordered baseline and its leaf
permutation, all entries in Equation (3) can be computed in O(N 2 ) time. In exact arithmetic,
these entries equal the corresponding full-tree BME differences.
Proof. The balanced-distance table costs O(N 2 ). For each of O(N ) coordinates, pruning its
associated component and traversing the two remaining half-trees visits O(N ) edges. The SPR
recurrence evaluates the score at each edge, and the coordinate mapping selects the appropriate
values without evaluating the candidate trees independently. The mapping costs at most O(N )
per row. The recurrence preserves the Pauplin score difference at each traversal step, so selecting
a mapped edge gives Equation (3).

    The proposition concerns the coordinate neighborhood of the current labeling. It does not
assert that this neighborhood is the entire SPR neighborhood, or that coordinate descent finds a
global BME optimum.

2.5    Search strategy
The full-tree CPU optimizer evaluates coordinate differences, ranks improving rows, and forms
candidate batches using disjoint path corridors as a conflict filter. It then evaluates the combined
candidate tree and backtracks to a smaller batch if the score does not improve. The acceptance
decision uses the joint objective rather than the sum of individual predicted improvements. When
the current coordinate neighborhood is exhausted, the optimizer changes the ordered labeling
and searches again. Its full-tree configuration also scans remaining unrooted SPR moves before
declaring SPR-local convergence. An optional restart stage applies random perturbations and
resumes descent while retaining the best tree found. Only accepted descent updates and the
retained best score are monotone. The perturbed working trajectory need not be.
    An exact minibatch search can alternate skeletons at different scales. A global skeleton
rearranges the backbone, local windows expose detailed structure, and overlapping or paired
windows propose changes between regions. Each window follows four steps:

   1. Select fixed components from the current tree, including an oriented outside component for
      a proper subtree.

   2. Form the exact condensed distance matrix in Equation (7).

   3. Evaluate skeleton coordinate changes using Corollary 2.4, and check the selected update
      using Equation (11).

   4. Commit an improving candidate, graft the components back, and update summaries before
      selecting or evaluating the next window.

These steps preserve the objective being optimized under the assumptions above. They do not
prove that a particular finite window schedule explores every tree or reaches every improving
move. Nor does an infinite number of batches alone establish such a result without assumptions
on which proposals can occur and be accepted.

2.6   Parallel evaluation and computational cost
Equation (4) has an independent recurrence for each fixed column. Assigning columns to threads
lets neighboring threads access consecutive entries while following the same traversal order. The
top-down correction for column w follows its ancestor path. Depth-first entry intervals determine
                                                                              P
the next child without a traversal stack. Its total work over columns is O( w depth(w)), bounded
by O(N 2 ) and reduced to O(N log N ) for trees of logarithmic height.
    A full balanced-distance table stores approximately 4N 2 entries. The column-tiled variant
retains the internal-to-leaf block and reconstructs required internal rows on demand. This reduces
that table to approximately N 2 entries, while the input matrix and other buffers still contribute
to total memory. A row-wise reduction can return the best coordinate per row without storing
the rectangular gradient output. Columns and SPR rows can also be distributed across GPUs.
Appendix B gives the dependency and workspace arguments.
    For a skeleton with K leaves, a coordinate sweep costs O(K 2 ) once its distances are available.
This does not make arbitrary distance construction independent of N . Direct evaluation of one
condensed pair can require |Sa ||Sb | original distances. Explicit leaf weights use O(N ) entries, and
constructing exact condensed distances can dominate runtime for large component pairs. When

The main evaluation distinguishes three workloads. First, small-tree tests compare coordinate
differences and condensed differences against independent evaluations of Equation (1). Second,
CPU optimization experiments compare final scores and wall times from identical starting trees.
Third, gradient benchmarks measure parallel sweep throughput, independently of convergence. The
large embedding-based scaling workload is distinct from sequence-distance refinement. Appendix C
separately reports sequence benchmarks of an implementation using approximate proposals and
exact full-tree acceptance calculations. Those timings do not measure a minibatch search using
exact condensed distances throughout.

4     Results
4.1     Agreement with independent objective calculations
Table 1 summarizes the reported numerical checks. The largest discrepancy in the CPU coordinate
tests is 3.553 × 10−14 , and the condensation tests agree to 1.564 × 10−13 . These are observed
absolute errors on the tested cases, not universal error bounds. Rooted-vector round trips recover
all 1,000 tested vectors at N = 40. Unrooted round trips preserve the split sets in a further 1,000
cases. Exhaustive rooted-tree enumeration at N = 4, . . . , 7 checks 11,460 encodings.
    Independent enumeration also identifies the scope of the coordinate neighborhood. On
30 random trees at each tested size from N = 8 to 20, a single ordered orientation covers
approximately 27–33% of distinct SPR neighbors. Two orientations together cover approximately
52–56%. Neither constitutes an exhaustive SPR search. The full CPU optimizer’s additional SPR
scan is therefore material: independent enumeration found no improving SPR move in 90 returned
trees at N = 10, 16, 24. This observation concerns that tested configuration, not the minibatch
variant.

4.2     CPU optimization
Both methods recover the reference topology in all 60 noiseless trials. On the 60 noisy trials,
Phylo2Vec obtains two lower BME scores, 57 ties, and one higher score than the in-memory
reference. Thus it matches or improves the reference objective in 119 of 120 cases overall. A better
BME score need not imply a smaller RF distance when the input distances are noisy. For example,
the N = 200 noisy comparison has a lower mean reference RF error despite one BME-score win
for Phylo2Vec.
    At N = 200, the noiseless Apple M5 comparison requires 63.6 gradient evaluations on average,
versus 211.0 reference SPR iterations. The corresponding mean times are 18.14 and 75.46 ms.
Table 2 gives the largest-size timings for both processors. Supplementary Tables 4 and 5 give
results at all sizes. External FastME comparisons at the same five sizes produce identical final
unrooted splits and BME differences below 10−8 in the reported checks. These checks support the
reference implementation on those inputs without making the in-memory timings measurements
of the external executable.

4.3   Gradient throughput and memory
At N = 4,000, a reported full-gradient evaluation takes 6.219 ms on one H100, compared with
701.375 ms for its CPU reference. At N = 40,000, distributing the full-table computation across
eight H100s reduces time from 816.34 to 158.36 ms, a factor of 5.15. The SPR stage alone
falls from 756.26 to 98.28 ms. Column tiling reduces storage at the cost of recomputation: the
reported one-GPU memory requirement falls from 31,142 to 13,614 MB, while the eight-GPU
tiled evaluation takes 1,032.83 ms at this size. Thus the reduced-memory variant is useful when
capacity, rather than latency, is limiting.
    Distributed and streamed configurations extend a single full-tree sweep to N = 380,000, taking
205.450 s with 72,946 MB reported per GPU on eight H100s. This is a sweep benchmark, not
the time to infer a converged tree. For the embedding-based workload with N = 10,000,000 and
K = 20,000, reported average condensation and gradient times are 10.20 and 48.57 ms per batch
on eight H100s. Multiplying their sum by 667 batches gives the reported epoch estimate of 39.20 s.
It excludes any additional search work not represented by those two timings and does not establish
a sequence-refinement time at that scale. The complete scaling tables are in Appendix G.


5     Discussion
The component identity provides the mathematical basis for evaluating local skeleton changes
against a global BME objective. The unchanged internal terms form a constant, and the remaining
terms are evaluated using exact balanced distances between components. This principle is
established BME structure. Its role here is to make explicit which data a minibatch evaluator
must preserve and to connect reduced skeleton optimization to Phylo2Vec coordinates and parallel
execution.
    The distinction between objective evaluation and search is essential. The distance matrix
may itself be estimated, and the search may select only a small subset of candidates. Within
the exact formulation, every evaluated coordinate difference is nevertheless the full-tree BME
difference for that candidate. Conversely, a correct algebraic identity does not remove floating-point
error or establish global convergence. Changing leaf labels enlarges the collection of coordinate
neighborhoods available to the optimizer, but the tested two-orientation schedule alone is not an
exhaustive SPR search.
    The main computational tradeoff is between reuse and reconstruction. Dense tables permit
rapid sweeps but require quadratic memory. Column tiling trades memory for repeated arithmetic.
Skeletons reduce the dimension of the optimization problem, but exact construction of their
distances can retain a dependence on the full leaf set. This becomes particularly visible in
nonlinear sequence-distance refinement and in candidates involving large condensed components.
Bilinear embedding benchmarks therefore establish a different scaling regime from the sequence
experiments.
    The empirical results support faster evaluation and competitive CPU optimization on the
tested instances. They do not establish an asymptotic bound on the number of optimization
sweeps, statistical superiority across data-generating models, or a universal numerical error bound.
The supplementary sequence comparisons use approximate candidate distances followed by full-
tree acceptance calculations and have no reported replicate uncertainty at the largest sizes. An
end-to-end sequence benchmark using exact condensed distances for every candidate evaluation
remains to be measured. Further comparisons should use matched distance matrices, identical
starting trees, explicit timing boundaries, and repeated runs, with the exact implementation
revision attached to each result.

6    Conclusion
Phylo2Vec coordinates provide a structured interface to exact, quadratic-time BME move eval-
uation. Exact balanced distances extend the same evaluator to reduced skeletons, preserving
the full-tree change for every valid skeleton coordinate update. With these exact distances,
minibatching restricts the set of candidate rearrangements while retaining the BME objective
for each evaluated candidate. The computational cost depends on both the skeleton size and the
construction of exact component distances. These identities support parallel tree search with
exact objective differences, subject to numerical rounding, without implying a globally optimal
search result.

A     Ordered representation and coordinate mapping
A.1    Linear-time decoding
For vi ≤ i, initialize a component-root array by r[x] = x. Process i = N − 2, . . . , 0. At step i,
join the components with roots r[vi ] and r[i + 1] at a new node m = 2N − 2 − i, then replace
r[vi ] by m. Before this step, the active components have distinct minimum labels 0, . . . , i + 1.
Joining the two specified components removes minimum label i + 1 and preserves minimum label
vi . Induction therefore establishes the invariant and the correctness of the construction. Each step
uses constant work, giving an O(N ) decoder. This argument depends on the ordered condition
and does not apply unchanged to a general vector with vi > i.

A.2    Relabeling a decoded tree
Proposition A.1 (Ordered representation after relabeling). Given a decoded rooted binary
tree, a parent-before-child ordering of its internal nodes constructs an ordered vector and a leaf
permutation in O(N ) time. The represented labeled tree and its BME score are unchanged.

Proof. Give the root temporary label zero and initialize a counter t = 1. At each internal node,
choose an ordering (a, b) of its children. Pass the node’s label to a, give b label t, record vt−1 as
the inherited label, and increment t. Each newly issued label follows a unique chain of inheriting
children to a leaf. Every leaf receives exactly one label, so the resulting labels form a permutation
of 0, . . . , N − 1.
    The inherited label was issued at an ancestor and is smaller than t, hence vt−1 ≤ t − 1. To
verify decoding, process the recorded splits in reverse order. All descendants of a node were
assigned their new labels later than that node, so both child components have already been
assembled when its recorded merge is reached. The merge therefore recreates that node’s original
clade. Induction reconstructs the entire tree. Permuting both axes of D by the inverse leaf-label
map preserves all terms in Equation (1). Each vertex is processed a constant number of times.

    The implementation chooses the child order from the minimum original leaf label in each
child component, with a second orientation reversing that choice. These are two deterministic
coordinate systems for the same current tree. Their existence proves representational coverage
after suitable relabeling, not coverage by a particular optimizer trajectory. For general candidate
vectors, the implementation also uses a tree-to-vector encoder. Its unrooted version first inserts a
degree-two root on the edge incident to leaf zero, so round-trip correctness is assessed by unrooted
splits rather than root placement.

B     Parallel dependencies and workspace
B.1    Column ownership and ancestor traversal
For a fixed column w, the bottom-up update in Equation (4) reads only earlier entries of that
column. Thus the recurrence has no cross-column dependency once its initial entries are available.
The implementation separates the necessary transpose from the recurrence rather than writing
both triangular copies concurrently. Top-down correction visits ancestors of w in order from the
computational root. If a node’s children have contiguous depth-first intervals, comparing the entry
time of w with their interval boundary selects the child containing w. Only the current node and
the fixed target are required as traversal state. The initial degree-three root update uses the other
two root branches.

B.2    Autonomous row reconstruction
Store the balanced distances from internal vertices to leaves. For a requested row w, these entries
supply its leaf values. For leaf w, the leaf values instead come directly from D. Average child
entries bottom-up to fill internal positions, then overwrite ancestors of w top-down using the
sibling and parent directions. Initialize the degree-three root correction explicitly and set the
diagonal to zero.
    For an internal position u in a disjoint component, the bottom-up average is the required
balanced distance by linearity. For a descendant u of w, the stored leaf values already use the
component at w directed away from that descendant, so averaging within the descendant gives the
correct value. Ancestors of w require the complementary direction and are supplied by the top-
down overwrite. These cases exhaust the row. The reconstruction costs O(N + depth(w)) = O(N ).
Reconstructing a constant number of rows per pruned component therefore retains quadratic
sweep complexity. The approximately fourfold reduction concerns the balanced-distance table
only.

B.3    Traversal stack size
When a traversal reaches two outward components, push the larger component first and the
smaller second on a last-in-first-out stack. The smaller component is processed next. Each extra
pending larger component is associated with descent into a component at most half the size of
its predecessor. Hence the number of simultaneously pending components is logarithmic in the
number of tree vertices. Including the active traversal state gives a safe bound ⌊log2 (2N − 2)⌋ + 2.
This is a workspace bound, not a statement that the traversal visits fewer vertices.

C     Sequence-refinement variant and recorded benchmarks
C.1    Implementation used for the recorded measurements
The sequence implementation used for the measurements in this appendix differs from the exact-
distance search described in Section 2.5. For trees larger than 2,048 leaves, it caps the weighted
leaf lists used for proposal distances at 64 entries per component. These lists are called profiles in
the implementation. Above the cap, unresolved subtree mass is assigned to representative leaves,
producing an approximate matrix D     e cap . For smaller trees, the proposal calculation is uncapped.
    The approximate matrix guides the inner skeleton search. Before accepting an update,
the implementation recomputes affected condensed distances involving truncated components
using every leaf contribution. Distances for untruncated pairs can be reused. It then evaluates
Equation (11), with FP64 accumulation and a numerical acceptance margin of 10−6 . Outside
weights are rebuilt from the current tree before each window, and ancestor summaries are updated
after a commit. The acceptance calculation therefore evaluates the full-tree BME change, subject
to rounding. It does not make the earlier proposal gradients exact, and the numerical margin is
not a proved floating-point error bound.
    These measurements characterize that implementation variant. They are not timings for
constructing exact condensed distances and using them throughout every minibatch search.

C.2    Sequence data and comparison protocol
The recorded comparisons use the RNASim benchmark associated with PASTA [9], with simulated
sequences and a known reference tree. The reported alignments have 1,596 columns. The minibatch
comparison uses gap-aware JC69 distances. Selected full-tree experiments also use the eigenvalue-
based distance approach [10], which changes the input objective and is identified separately.
The CPU tools are 64-thread FastME 2.1.6 and VeryFastTree 4.0.5 [5, 8]. VeryFastTree’s
minimum-evolution refinement is run without its likelihood-refinement phase. Refinement time is
reported separately from starting-tree construction. The minibatch times include the final full-tree
acceptance calculation and sequential maintenance of the outside distances.

C.3    Recorded refinement results

topology. At N = 50,000, the minibatch method returns 4,950 incorrect splits in 16.84 s, compared
with 4,980 in 313.53 s. At N = 100,000, the corresponding results are 8,802 in 104.45 s and 8,857
in 593.50 s. The refinement-time ratios are 18.6 and 5.7 respectively. These exclude the shared
initialization, which takes 63.01 and 179.03 s. Adding that initialization to both methods gives
approximately 4.72-fold and 2.73-fold ratios, rather than the refinement-only ratios.

D     Truncation and stale outside-distance counterexamples
Two constructions illustrate the need for exact condensed distances and current outside information.
The first concerns the approximate-proposal variant in Appendix C. On a 4,102-leaf tree containing
a balanced 4,096-leaf component, an adversarial split distance can give a capped proposal change
of −0.25 while its true full-profile change is +0.2421875 for a cap of 64, or +0.234375 for a cap of
128. The proposal sign alone is therefore insufficient. Full-profile acceptance rejects both changes.
    Separately, consider two disjoint windows whose internal rearrangements change their root-to-
leaf weight vectors from pA , pB to pA + δpA , pB + δpB . With a fixed path coefficient κ between their
attachment points, their cross contribution contains κp⊤  A DAB pB . The jointly changed contribution
includes the additional term
                                            κ δp⊤A DAB δpB ,

which is absent from the sum of two changes computed against the original surroundings. In a
1,540-leaf test with two 768-leaf windows, changes −0.0032945313 and −0.0254489835 computed
against frozen surroundings combine to an actual increase of +0.0505258348. Rebuilding the
outside profile after the first commit makes the other proposal positive: +0.0538203660 or
+0.0759748183, depending on the processing order. It is then rejected. These values come from
controlled CPU replay of the acceptance and window-controller paths. They do not constitute a
native-GPU concurrency test.
